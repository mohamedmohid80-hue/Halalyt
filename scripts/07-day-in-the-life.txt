Right now, your day probably starts with an alarm you snooze three times. You check your phone before your eyes are even open. Breakfast is whatever you can grab on the way out. You sit in traffic, sit at a desk, sit on the sofa, and you go to sleep long after midnight with a screen two inches from your face. And somehow, you're still tired.

Now rewind fourteen hundred years, to Madinah, in the time of the Prophet, sallallahu alayhi wa sallam. No alarm clock. No electricity. No fridge, no car, no phone. Just mud brick houses, date palms and the desert sun.

That sounds like a hard life. And in many ways it was. But when you look at how the Companions actually lived, through authentic hadith, their day had things most of us would love to have. So what did Muslims in Madinah actually do all day? Let's go through it, from before sunrise until the lights went out.

It starts before dawn. There's no alarm, just the call of the adhan carrying over quiet streets, and the sound of people waking up for Fajr. Fajr was prayed so early that, according to Aishah, may Allah be pleased with her, the women who prayed it at the masjid would walk back home wrapped in their cloaks, and nobody could even recognise them in the dark.

And here's something most people don't know. After Fajr, the Prophet, sallallahu alayhi wa sallam, would often stay sitting in his place until the sun had fully risen. The Companions would sit with him and talk, sometimes about their lives before Islam, and they would laugh, and he would smile. The day didn't start with a rush. It started with prayer, people, and laughter.

Then breakfast. Don't imagine a big spread. For most families, food was simple, and sometimes very little. Aishah said there were months when no fire was lit in the Prophet's house to cook anything. They lived on dates and water. Barley bread, milk from their animals and dates were the normal meal.

Next, work. The morning was when Madinah came alive. Many of the Ansar worked on their date palm farms, watering the trees and climbing them to pollinate and harvest. Others worked in the market. When Abdur-Rahman ibn Awf arrived in Madinah with nothing, the first thing he asked was, show me the market. He started trading, and became one of the richest men in the city.

Water didn't come out of a tap. It came from wells, carried home in leather skins. Wells were so important that Uthman, may Allah be pleased with him, bought a well called Rumah and gave its water to the Muslims for free.

Then came the hottest part of the day. And people did something that sounds strange to us now. They took a short midday nap, called the qaylulah. One Companion said, after Jumuah we would take our qaylulah and eat lunch. Resting at midday wasn't laziness. It was part of the day.

Now, the homes. The Prophet's own rooms were made of mud brick with roofs of palm branches, and they were so low that a man could reach up and touch the ceiling. There was almost no furniture. People sat and slept on mats on the floor.

In the afternoon, when the heat dropped, there was time for something we don't expect. Sport. The Prophet, sallallahu alayhi wa sallam, organised horse races. He passed by a group of men practising archery and told them, shoot, and I am with you. Running, racing and wrestling were part of normal life, and being strong and fit was something to be proud of.

Then Maghrib, and the evening meal. Families ate together, sitting on the floor, from one shared plate.

And then something else that would feel very strange to us. Night came fast, and it was dark. Aishah said that in those days the houses had no lamps. So after Isha, people didn't stay up. The Prophet, sallallahu alayhi wa sallam, disliked sleeping before Isha and disliked talking after it. People went to sleep early, and many woke up again in the last part of the night to pray.

So think about the difference. We have alarms, lights, fridges, cars and phones, all made to give us more time. They had none of it. But their day had a rhythm we've lost. They woke early, started the day with prayer and company, worked hard in the morning, rested at midday, moved their bodies in the afternoon, ate together in the evening, and slept when it got dark.

They didn't have more things. They had a better rhythm. And the good news is, most of it is still free.
